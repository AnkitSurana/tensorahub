Summary: Discrete Probability Distributions

A short recap of the discrete-distributions arc: finding probabilities without running an experiment, using the binomial formula to handle the general case, and using cumulative probability to answer "at most this many" questions.

Probability without an experiment (from Chapter 9, Probability Without an Experiment)

We started with a question: can we find probability without running the experiment? The answer was yes. Two basic rules from Chapter 2, Probability Recap, were enough on their own:

  • The multiplication rule, for independent events happening together.
  • The addition rule, for mutually exclusive outcomes.

Applying both to the bag game (3 red, 2 blue, drawn 4 times with replacement, X = number of red balls) gave the full theoretical distribution of X:

Observed vs theoretical distribution

Back in Chapter 5, Probability Distributions, we had gotten an observed distribution by asking 75 real people to play the game and counting how many landed on each value of X. Comparing that observed distribution to the theoretical one from Chapter 9 (Figure 1):

Figure 1. The observed distribution (left, from 75 real players) and the theoretical distribution (right, from the multiplication and addition rules) side by side. The shapes are strikingly similar.

The two distributions are close but not identical. That is because the observed one came from only 75 experiments. The more you run, the closer the observed values get to the theoretical ones. Run infinitely many, and the two would match exactly.

The binomial distribution (from Chapter 10, Binomial Distribution)

We then generalised beyond our specific bag. What if the bag had a different mix, or you drew a different number of balls? The general question is: what is the probability of getting exactly r red balls in N draws, if each single draw has probability p of being red?

The full distribution for X = number of red balls drawn is:

xP(X = x)
0NC0 · p0 · (1 − p)N
1NC1 · p1 · (1 − p)N−1
2NC2 · p2 · (1 − p)N−2
3NC3 · p3 · (1 − p)N−3
……
NNCN · pN · (1 − p)0

In one line, for any value r from 0 to N:

P(X = r) = NCr · pr · (1 − p)N−r

This is the binomial distribution. It works whenever three things are all true:

  1. The total number of trials, N, is fixed in advance.
  2. Each trial has only two possible outcomes (success or failure).
  3. The probability of success, p, stays the same in every trial.

The bag game meets all three: N = 4 draws is fixed, each draw is red or blue, and putting the ball back keeps p = 0.6 the same every time. Once those three conditions hold, the formula gives you the exact probability of any count r, with no experiment needed.

Cumulative probability (from Chapter 11, Cumulative Probability Distributions)

Many real questions do not ask "what is the probability of exactly this value?" They ask "what is the probability of at most this many?" The answer to that kind of question is the cumulative probability, written F(x):

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

To find F(x), just add up every individual probability from 0 up to and including x. For the bag game, F(2), the probability of drawing 2 or fewer red balls, works out like this:

F(2) = P(X = 0) + P(X = 1) + P(X = 2)
F(2) = 0.0256 + 0.1536 + 0.3456= 0.5248

So about half the time, a player of the bag game will draw 2 or fewer red balls.

Now that discrete distributions are behind us, the next session moves to continuous random variables. There, the value can fall anywhere along a range instead of on a fixed list, so the way probability is expressed has to change to fit.
