Bayes' Theorem

How new evidence updates a probability, from prior belief to posterior belief, and the cricket example that ties it together.

Prior probability and posterior probability

A prior probability is what you believe before you get any new information. A posterior probability is what you believe after you add that new information. Bayes' theorem is the rule that connects the two.

In your childhood, you play outside on 20 days out of 30 days in a month. What is the prior probability that you play on a random day?

With no other information, use the plain count of days:

2030is about 0.67

Now suppose it is going to rain today. Rain almost always stops you from playing, so this new fact should change your belief. Say that, looking specifically at rainy days in the past, you actually went out to play on only about 10% of them. This 0.10 is not calculated from the 20/30 figure above, it is a separate count: out of all the rainy days, how many of those specific days did you still play on. It is the same kind of calculation as the prior, days played divided by days total, just applied to a smaller group (rainy days only) instead of every day.

So the posterior probability that you play, given that it will rain, is about 0.10. The prior was 0.67. The posterior, once you account for the rain, is much lower, because rainy days are simply a group of days on which you played far less often than on an average day.

Out of every 5 emails you get, 1 is spam. What is the prior probability that a new email is spam?

With no other information:

15= 0.20

Now suppose the email contains the word "Viagra". Say that, looking specifically at all the past emails containing that word, 70% of them turned out to be spam. This 0.70 is not calculated from the 1/5 figure above, it is a separate count: out of all the emails containing "Viagra", how many of those specific emails were spam. Again, it is the same kind of calculation as the prior, spam emails divided by total emails, just applied to a smaller group (emails with that word) instead of every email.

So the posterior probability that the email is spam, given that it contains "Viagra", is about 0.70, noticeably higher than the 0.20 prior. This is the same idea a spam filter uses: start from a prior, then update it once a clue like a certain word appears.

From conditional probability to Bayes' theorem

Recall the conditional probability rule from Chapter 2, Probability Recap, in the "Marginal, joint, and conditional probability" section:

P(A | B)= P(A ∩ B)P(B)

The same rule also works the other way round:

P(B | A)= P(A ∩ B)P(A)

Rearrange the second rule and you get another way to write the joint probability:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) · P(A)

Now put this into the first rule in place of P(A ∩ B). That gives you Bayes' theorem:

P(A | B)= P(B | A) · P(A)P(B)

Each part of this formula has a name:

  • P(A) is the prior. Your belief about A before you see any evidence.
  • P(B | A) is the likelihood. How likely the evidence B is, if A is true.
  • P(B) is the evidence. How likely B is overall, across every case.
  • P(A | B) is the posterior. Your updated belief about A, now that you know B happened.

The cricket example

You may have heard commentary like this: "India wins 70% of matches when Tendulkar scores a century." That is a conditional probability, and Bayes' theorem is how you can work it out properly.

Say India plays 100 matches. It wins 60 and loses 40. In those same 100 matches, Sachin Tendulkar scores a century in 12 and does not score a century in the other 88. On top of that, out of the 60 matches India wins, Sachin scores a century in 10 of them. Out of the 40 matches India loses, Sachin scores a century in only 2.

Here is the two way table for this data:

India WinsIndia LosesTotal
Century10212
No Century503888
Total6040100

These are match counts out of 100, not probabilities yet. Divide by 100 to turn any cell or total into a probability.

Here is the same 100 matches as a picture (Figure 1), one small square per match, filled in order (win with century, win without, lose with century, lose without):

Win, century (10)   Win, no century (50)   Lose, century (2)   Lose, no century (38)

From this table, three basic probabilities:

P(Win) = 60100 = 0.6 P(Century) = 12100 = 0.12 P(Win ∩ Century) = 10100 = 0.10

Joint probability, worked out two ways

You can reach that same joint probability, P(Win ∩ Century) = 0.10, by conditioning on either event first. Condition on Century first:

P(Win ∩ Century)= P(Win | Century)·P(Century) = 1012· 12100 =0.10

Or condition on Win first:

P(Win ∩ Century)= P(Century | Win)·P(Win) = 1060· 60100 =0.10

Both paths give the same number, because both are just two ways of describing the same 10 matches. Since they are equal, you can set them equal to each other:

P(Win | Century) · P(Century) = P(Century | Win) · P(Win)

Divide both sides by P(Century), and you get Bayes' theorem again, this time built directly from the table instead of from abstract letters:

P(Win | Century)= P(Century | Win) · P(Win)P(Century)

What is the probability that India wins, given that Sachin has scored a century?

The fast way: look at the Century row only, since that is the condition. Out of the 12 matches with a century, India won 10 of them.

P(Win | Century)= 1012is about 0.83

This matches the Bayes' theorem calculation above: 0.10 divided by 0.12 is also about 0.83.

What is the probability that India loses, given that Sachin does not score a century?

Look at the No Century row only. Out of 88 matches with no century, India lost 38.

P(Lose | No Century)= 3888is about 0.43

Check with Bayes' theorem:

P(No Century | Lose) = 3840 P(Lose) = 40100 P(No Century) = 88100
P(Lose | No Century)= 0.95 · 0.40.88 = 0.380.88 is about 0.43

Same answer again. Notice that P(Win | Century) plus P(Lose | Century) should add up to 1, since a century match either ends in a win or a loss. Here that is 0.83 plus 0.17, which checks out.
