Probability Without an Experiment

Why running an experiment every time is not practical, and how combinatorics plus the addition and multiplication rules build a full probability distribution without one.

The problem with needing an experiment

In the last session, we found probabilities by running an experiment, in Chapter 5, Probability Distributions, in the "Real people play the game" section. We asked 75 people to play the bag game, built a histogram from their results, and turned that histogram into a probability distribution.

This works, but it is a lot of effort. It also raises a few problems. What if you do not have 75 people available to test the game? What if you are short on time? And what if you want to know the answer for a different bag, say one with 2 red and 3 blue balls, or 1 red and 4 blue? Running a fresh 75-person experiment for every possible bag is not practical.

Is there a way to get the probability without running the experiment at all?

Using the multiplication rule instead

Recall the multiplication rule from Chapter 2, Probability Recap, in the "Multiplication rule (for "AND")" section: if two events are independent, the probability of both happening is the product of their individual probabilities.

Our bag has 3 red balls and 2 blue balls, and the ball is replaced after every draw, so every draw is independent of the last. The probability of red on a single draw is:

35= 0.6

What is the probability of drawing a red ball on both of 2 draws (2 trials)?

The draws are independent, so multiply the probability of red by itself:

0.6 × 0.6= 0.36

No experiment needed. Knowing just the probability of red on one draw, and that the draws are independent, was enough to work this out directly.

This is the basic idea behind finding probability without an experiment: the multiplication rule alone gets us the probability of every draw coming up red. But our actual game asks a slightly different question: what is the probability of getting, say, exactly 3 red balls out of the 4 draws, in any order, not just 3 draws in a row? For that, the reds do not all have to come first, so we need one more ingredient alongside multiplication.

Handling "any order": combinatorics, multiplication, and addition together

Our actual game has 4 draws, and we want the probability of each possible value of X, the number of red balls, in any order. Recall from Chapter 4, Random Variables, in the "Listing all the outcomes" and "Turning the outcomes into a random variable" sections, that the 4 draws split into these groups by order:

  • X = 0: 1 arrangement (BBBB)
  • X = 1: 4 arrangements (RBBB, BRBB, BBRB, BBBR)
  • X = 2: 6 arrangements
  • X = 3: 4 arrangements
  • X = 4: 1 arrangement (RRRR)

Take X = 3 as an example, since that is the question we were left with earlier. One of its 4 arrangements is blue, then red, red, red. Its probability is:

P(BRRR) = 0.4 × 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6= 0.0864

The other 3 arrangements for X = 3 (red first then blue, and so on) each work out to this same 0.0864, since multiplying the same four numbers together gives the same answer regardless of order. These 4 arrangements are mutually exclusive, only one can happen in a single game, so add them together:

4 × 0.0864= 0.3456

So the probability of getting exactly 3 red balls, in any order, out of the 4 draws is 0.3456. That is the real answer to the question left open earlier, not the probability of 3 draws in a row.

Now take X = 1 as a second example. Each of its 4 arrangements has the same probability, since multiplying 0.6 and 0.4 together gives the same answer regardless of order:

P(RBBB) = 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4= 0.0384

A player can only draw one of these 4 arrangements in a single game, never two at once, so they are mutually exclusive. That is exactly the condition for the addition rule, so we add all 4 arrangements together to get P(X = 1):

4 × 0.0384= 0.1536

Multiplication handles the "and" inside one arrangement (independent draws). Addition handles the "or" across different arrangements. The reason we add here is that the arrangements are mutually exclusive, only one of them can happen in a single game, not because they are independent of each other.

Using the same method, work out P(X = 2): 6 arrangements, each with 2 red draws and 2 blue draws.

Each arrangement has the same probability, 2 factors of 0.6 and 2 factors of 0.4, in some order:

0.6 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.4= 0.0576

There are 6 such mutually exclusive arrangements, so add this probability 6 times:

6 × 0.0576= 0.3456

Working out every value of X the same way gives the full theoretical probability distribution, calculated entirely without running the experiment (Figure 1):

Figure 1. The theoretical probability distribution, calculated entirely without an experiment.

Notice the shape in Figure 1: X = 2 and X = 3 have almost the same, and by far the highest, probabilities, while X = 0, X = 1, and X = 4 are all noticeably lower. That makes sense here, since the bag favors red (0.6 vs. 0.4), so results with an even mix or slightly more red than blue are the most likely outcomes overall.

Observed probability distribution vs. theoretical probability distribution

Compare this to the distribution we found by actually running the experiment, back in Chapter 5, Probability Distributions, in the "Real people play the game" and "From frequency to probability" sections, from 75 real people playing the game:

Observed P(X)0.0270.1600.3470.3330.133
Theoretical P(X)0.0260.1540.3460.3460.130

The observed and theoretical values are close but not identical, for example 0.333 versus 0.346 at X = 3. Why would that be?

The observed distribution came from only 75 people. Recall from Chapter 2, Probability Recap, in the "Empirical vs. theoretical probability" section, that empirical probability gets closer to theoretical probability as the number of trials grows. With more players, say thousands instead of 75, the observed distribution would settle in even closer to this theoretical one.

This is what "probability without an experiment" really means: given just the probability of red on one draw, combinatorics and the multiplication and addition rules together can build the entire probability distribution, for any bag composition, without ever needing a single real player. Generalizing this exact method is what the binomial distribution does, next.
