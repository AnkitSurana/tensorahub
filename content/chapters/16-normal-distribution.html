The Normal Distribution

The most common continuous distribution: the bell curve. Its two parameters, its 1-2-3 rule for reading off probabilities without any calculus, and enough worked examples to make it feel automatic.

Why the normal distribution matters

The normal distribution, also known as the Gaussian distribution or simply the bell curve, is the most commonly used continuous probability distribution. Every real-world variable in the list below tends to follow one:

  • Heights of a group of adult men or women.
  • IQ scores in a large enough population.
  • The weight of a mass-produced item, like a Coca-Cola bottle or a loaf of bread from the same factory.
  • Measurement errors when the same thing is measured many times.

The pattern is so common that when a continuous variable has "most of its values clustered around a middle, with a symmetric spread on both sides," the normal distribution is almost always the first thing to try.

Shape and parameters

A normal distribution is fully described by just two numbers:

  • Its mean, written μ (the Greek letter mu), which fixes the centre of the curve.
  • Its standard deviation, written σ (the Greek letter sigma), which fixes how spread out it is.

The curve is symmetric about μ. The mean, the median, and the mode all coincide there, at the peak. The tails on either side become very thin very quickly, which is what makes the 1-2-3 rule below work so cleanly.

The 1-2-3 rule

For any normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ, three probabilities are worth memorising:

  1. About 68% of values lie within 1σ of the mean, meaning between μ − σ and μ + σ.
  2. About 95% of values lie within 2σ of the mean, meaning between μ − 2σ and μ + 2σ.
  3. About 99.7% of values lie within 3σ of the mean, meaning between μ − 3σ and μ + 3σ.

This is called the 1-2-3 rule (or the 68-95-99.7 rule), and it is shown visually in Figure 1:

Figure 1. A normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ. The three brackets at the top mark the 1σ, 2σ, and 3σ ranges, containing about 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of the probability respectively.

Making the rule concrete: a loaf of bread

Suppose you buy a loaf of bread every day from the same bakery, and someone tells you the weight of a loaf is normally distributed with mean 100 g and standard deviation 1 g. The 1-2-3 rule then says:

  1. About 68% of the loaves weigh between 99 g and 101 g (that is, within 1σ of 100).
  2. About 95% weigh between 98 g and 102 g (within 2σ of 100).
  3. About 99.7% weigh between 97 g and 103 g (within 3σ of 100).

Turning those percentages into a day-count:

0.68 × 7 days=~5 days per week
0.95 × 21 days=~20 days every 3 weeks
0.997 × 365 days=~364 days every year

So the loaves you buy will fall inside the tight 99 to 101 g band on about 5 days of a typical week, and only about 1 day a year will the loaf fall outside 97 to 103 g. The 1-2-3 rule turns an abstract "standard deviation" into concrete expectations you can check against reality.

Where you actually see normal distributions

To make the abstract concrete another way, 50 employees at an office were asked their height (in inches). The probability density of those heights was then plotted in Figure 2:

Figure 2. The probability density of self-reported heights from 50 employees. The shape is roughly bell-shaped, peaking near 65 inches, with tails on both sides. A larger sample would look even more like an ideal normal curve.

The shape is not perfectly smooth (50 people is a small sample), but the overall pattern is unmistakable: most heights sit near the middle, and the distribution tapers off symmetrically on either side. This is what "roughly normal" data looks like in practice.

Using the 1-2-3 rule to compute probabilities

The 1-2-3 rule, combined with the fact that the normal curve is symmetric about the mean, is enough to answer a lot of probability questions without any calculus at all. To see how, assume for the next three examples that a normal random variable X has mean μ = 35 and standard deviation σ = 5.

First, translate a few key positions on the x-axis into σ-multiples of the mean, since that is the language the 1-2-3 rule speaks:

μ − σ = 35 − 5 = 30 μ + σ = 35 + 5 = 40
μ − 2σ = 35 − 10 = 25 μ + 2σ = 35 + 10 = 45
μ − 3σ = 35 − 15 = 20 μ + 3σ = 35 + 15 = 50

What is P(25 ≤ X ≤ 45)?

The range 25 to 45 is exactly μ − 2σ to μ + 2σ. Reading straight off the 1-2-3 rule:

P(μ − 2σ ≤ X ≤ μ + 2σ)= 95%

No arithmetic beyond the rule itself was needed.

What is P(25 ≤ X ≤ 50)? This range is not symmetric about the mean, so the 1-2-3 rule does not apply directly.

The trick is to split the range at the mean (μ = 35), then use the fact that the curve is symmetric to get each half separately from the 1-2-3 rule.

Right half, from μ to μ + 3σ (that is, 35 to 50): the full μ − 3σ to μ + 3σ range covers 99.7% of the probability. By symmetry, the half of that range on the right of the mean covers exactly half of 99.7%:

P(μ ≤ X ≤ μ + 3σ) = 99.7% ÷ 2= 49.85%

Left half, from μ − 2σ to μ (that is, 25 to 35): by the same symmetry argument, this covers half of the 95% band:

P(μ − 2σ ≤ X ≤ μ) = 95% ÷ 2= 47.5%

The two halves cover different pieces of the whole 25 to 50 range with no overlap, so add them together:

P(25 ≤ X ≤ 50) = 49.85% + 47.5%= 97.35%

What is P(X < 40)? Note this is a one-sided range (everything below 40), not a symmetric one, so it needs the same "split at the mean" trick.

40 is μ + σ. Split "everything below 40" into two pieces at the mean:

Below the mean (X < μ = 35): the curve is symmetric about the mean, so exactly half the probability lies below it:

P(X < μ)= 50%

Between the mean and μ + σ (35 to 40): half of the 68% band:

P(μ ≤ X ≤ μ + σ) = 68% ÷ 2= 34%

Add the two pieces together:

P(X < 40) = 50% + 34%= 84%

Every problem of this type follows the same pattern: split the range at the mean if needed, look up each half in the 1-2-3 rule (using symmetry to halve a band when only one side is wanted), then add the pieces up. No calculus, no tables, no calculator.
