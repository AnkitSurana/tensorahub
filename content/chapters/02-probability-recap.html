Probability Recap

Basic definitions, event types, and the two main rules used later in probability and Bayes' theorem.

Core definitions

Probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely something is to happen. 0 means it will never happen, 1 means it is certain, and everything else falls somewhere in between. We write the probability of an event A as P(A).

An experiment is something you can repeat and observe. Rolling a die or tossing a coin are both examples. Each time you do the experiment once, that is called a trial. What you get from one trial is the result. An outcome is any result the experiment could give. The set of all possible outcomes is called the sample space. For one die roll, the sample space is {1,2,3,4,5,6}, so it has 6 outcomes. An event is any subset of the sample space. Nothing outside the sample space can happen.

An event with just one outcome, like rolling a 1, is called an elementary event. An event made of several outcomes, like rolling an even number ({2,4,6}), is called a compound event. An event that can never happen is the null event. Its probability is 0.

Event types

  • Mutually exclusive: two events cannot happen at the same time. For example, rolling a 1 and a 2 on one roll.
  • Exhaustive: the events together cover the whole sample space.
  • Independent: one event does not affect the other.
  • Complement: for event A, the complement is everything not in A. Its probability is 1 minus P(A).

You roll a die. Look at two events: "the number is 3 or less" and "the number is 4 or more". Are these mutually exclusive? Are they exhaustive too?

Yes to both. They share no outcome, so they are mutually exclusive. Together, {1,2,3} and {4,5,6} make up the whole sample space, so they are exhaustive too.

You toss a coin twice. Is getting heads on the first toss independent of getting heads on the second toss?

Yes. The coin has no memory. The first toss does not change the chances on the second toss.

A weather forecast says there is a 30% chance of rain today. So P(rain) = 0.3. What is the probability it does not rain?

Not raining is the complement of raining. Subtract from 1:

1-0.3=0.7

Empirical vs. theoretical probability

Empirical probability comes from actually running the experiment:

P(E)= times E occurrednumber of trials, N

For example, you roll a die 50 times and get a 2 nine times. That gives an empirical probability of:

P(2)= 950 =0.18

Theoretical probability does not need an experiment at all. It assumes every outcome is equally likely:

P(E)= favourable outcomessize of sample space
P(2)= 16 is about0.17

When you run more trials, empirical probability gets closer to theoretical probability. For example, in coin tosses, the share of heads moves closer to 0.5 as you toss more times.

There are three ways to work out a likelihood. The equal likelihood approach assumes every outcome is equally likely. The relative frequency approach uses real data. The judgemental approach uses expert judgement when you have neither of the above.

You roll a die 200 times. A 6 comes up 40 times. What are the empirical and theoretical values of P(6)?

Empirical uses the real count. Theoretical assumes a fair die.

Empirical= 40200 =0.20
Theoretical= 16 is about0.167

The gap between 0.20 and 0.167 should get smaller if you keep rolling. It is also an early sign the die might be a little biased.

Which approach fits this case: "the probability a new product launch succeeds"? Equal likelihood, relative frequency, or judgemental?

Judgemental. There is no reason to assume equal chances, like with a die. There is also no large set of past trials to use. So you rely on expert or market judgement instead.

Addition rule (for "OR")

For any two events A and B:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

P(A ∩ B) is the chance both happen. You subtract it once, because it would be counted twice otherwise. If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A ∩ B) = 0, so the rule becomes P(A) + P(B). Figure 1 shows why the overlap needs correcting for.

Figure 1. Events A and B as overlapping circles. Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap, A ∩ B, twice, once as part of A and once as part of B. Subtracting P(A ∩ B) once corrects for that.

You roll a die once. What is the probability of getting a 2 or a 5?

These two outcomes cannot both happen on one roll, so they are mutually exclusive. Just add the two probabilities:

16+ 16= 26= 13

You draw one card from a well shuffled 52 card deck. What is the probability of getting a king or a red card?

These are not mutually exclusive. Two red kings, the king of hearts and the king of diamonds, count in both groups. So that overlap has to be removed once.

P(king) = 452 P(red) = 2652 P(king ∩ red) = 252
452+ 2652- 252= 2852

You draw one card from a 52 card deck. What is the probability of getting a spade or a face card (J, Q, K)?

The 3 spade face cards (J, Q, K of spades) are counted in both groups. So you subtract that overlap once.

P(spade) = 1352 P(face) = 1252 P(spade ∩ face) = 352
1352+ 1252- 352= 2252

In a class of 40 students, 18 play chess, 15 play football, and 6 play both. What is the probability that a random student plays at least one of the two?

"At least one" means chess or football. Add the two groups, then remove the 6 students counted twice.

P(chess) = 1840 P(football) = 1540 P(both) = 640
1840+ 1540- 640= 2740= 0.675

Multiplication rule (for "AND")

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

If A and B are independent, P(B|A) is just P(B). So P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B). This also works for more than two independent events. You just multiply all of their probabilities together.

You toss a coin twice. What is the probability of getting heads both times?

The two tosses do not affect each other. Multiply the two probabilities:

12× 12= 14

You roll a die three times in a row. What is the probability of getting a 6 all three times?

Still independent, just with three events this time. Multiply all three:

16× 16× 16= 1216

You draw two cards from a deck, one after another, without putting the first one back. What is the probability that both are kings?

These events depend on each other. After one king is removed, only 3 kings are left in a 51 card deck for the second draw.

452× 351= 122652= 1221

A bag has 5 red marbles and 3 blue marbles, 8 in total. You draw two marbles without replacement. What is the probability that both are red?

These events depend on each other. After the first red marble is removed, 4 red marbles are left out of 7 total.

58× 47= 2056= 514
