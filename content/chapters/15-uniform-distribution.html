Uniform Distribution

The simplest continuous distribution: constant density across a range, why the area-under-PDF rule pins down the exact height, and why the PDF form is usually easier to read than the CDF form for spotting shape.

What a uniform distribution looks like

In a uniform continuous distribution, every possible value has the same probability density. No value is more or less likely than another within the range. Figure 1 shows a uniform PDF whose possible values run from 0 to 10:

Figure 1. A uniform PDF over 0 to 10. The height is some fixed value h, and the total area under the curve (the shaded rectangle) must equal 1.

Finding the height h

Recall from Chapter 14, Probability Density Functions, in the "The probability density function" section, that the total area under any PDF must equal 1, since it covers every possible outcome. That fact alone is enough to pin down h.

For the uniform PDF in Figure 1, what is the value of the probability density between X = 0 and X = 10?

The area under the PDF is the area of a rectangle with width 10 (from 0 to 10) and height h:

area = width × height = 10 × h

Since the area must equal 1:

10 × h = 1

Divide both sides by 10:

h= 110 = 0.1

So the probability density is 0.1 for every value between 0 and 10, and 0 everywhere else.

Cumulative probability from the uniform PDF

With the height known, we can now find the cumulative probability at any x by measuring the area under the PDF from the smallest possible value (0) up to that x. Figure 2 shows what F(0.5) looks like as an area:

Figure 2. The shaded slice from X = 0 to X = 0.5 is F(0.5). It is a thin rectangle of width 0.5 and height 0.1.

Using the uniform PDF from Figure 2, find F(0.5), the cumulative probability at X = 0.5.

F(0.5) is the area under the PDF from the smallest possible value (0) up to 0.5. That area is a rectangle with width 0.5 and height 0.1:

F(0.5) = width × height = 0.5 × 0.1= 0.05

The same reasoning works for any value of x in the range: F(x) is a rectangle of width x and height 0.1, so F(x) = 0.1 × x. For example, F(5) = 0.1 × 5 = 0.5, exactly halfway, which matches the intuition that half the range covers half the probability.

PDF or CDF: which one to use?

Both the PDF and the CDF describe the same variable's distribution. They carry the same information, just in different visual forms. So which one is more useful in practice?

For spotting the shape of a distribution at a glance, the PDF is usually easier. Compare the PDF and CDF forms of the same uniform distribution in Figure 3:

Figure 3. Left: PDF of a uniform distribution from -5 to 5 (a flat line at 0.1). Right: the same distribution's CDF (a straight ramp from 0 to 1). Both carry the same information; the PDF's flat line makes the uniformity easier to spot at a glance.

The PDF's flat line makes uniformity immediately obvious. The CDF's straight ramp carries the same information, but you have to notice its slope is constant to recognise it as uniform.

The gap is even wider for a symmetric distribution. Figure 4 shows the PDF and CDF of a symmetric bell-shaped variable:

Figure 4. Left: PDF of a symmetric bell-shaped distribution. Right: its CDF, an S-shaped curve. The bell's symmetry around 0 is obvious in the PDF, less so in the CDF.

The bell shape and its symmetry around 0 are easy to see in the PDF. The CDF's S shape also encodes the symmetry (as a reflection through its middle point), but that takes more work to notice.

Because of this, PDFs are used more often than CDFs in practice, especially for exploratory work where you first want to see what shape the distribution has. The CDF still has its uses, most importantly for reading off exact range probabilities using F(b) − F(a), as introduced in Chapter 11, Cumulative Probability Distributions, in the "Using cumulative probability to find a range" section.

Using the uniform PDF from earlier (Figures 1 and 2), what is the probability that X falls between 3 and 7?

The area under the PDF from 3 to 7 is a rectangle with width 4 (from 3 to 7) and height 0.1:

P(3 ≤ X ≤ 7) = 4 × 0.1= 0.4

The same range covers 4 out of the 10 total units of width, which is 40% of the whole range, matching the intuition for a uniform distribution: probability is proportional to how much of the range the interval covers.
