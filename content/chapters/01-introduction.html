Introduction

What this set of notes covers, and how the chapters connect to each other.

What this section covers

These notes cover the probability and statistics foundations from the AI & ML coursework: the basic rules of probability, how to update a belief once new evidence comes in, how to turn the outcome of an experiment into something you can compute with, and how to judge an uncertain situation, a game, a bet, or a business decision, using a single number.

These ideas come up constantly in machine learning: a spam filter updating its belief once it sees a suspicious word is Bayes' theorem, a model's predicted class is a random variable, and comparing two decisions under uncertainty usually comes down to expected value.

The chapters

  • 02 Probability Recap

    The basic vocabulary of probability: experiments, events, and sample spaces, the difference between empirical and theoretical probability, the addition and multiplication rules, and how to read marginal, joint, and conditional probability off a two-way table.
  • 03 Bayes' Theorem

    How a prior belief gets updated into a posterior belief once you see new evidence, derived from the conditional probability rule and worked through fully with a cricket example.
  • 04 Random Variables

    Turning the outcome of an experiment, like the colors of balls drawn from a bag, into a number you can actually work with, and why the right choice of random variable depends on what you care about.
  • 05 Probability Distributions

    Once outcomes are numbers, this covers finding how likely every possible value is, and showing that as a table, a chart, or an equation.
  • 06 Expected Value

    The single number that tells you what a random variable is worth "on average" over the long run, and how that number is used to judge whether a game, a bet, or an investment is worth it.
  • 07 Summary: Random Variables to Expected Value

    A short recap tying chapters 4 through 6 together in one place.

One running example

Chapters 4 through 7 all follow the same running example: a simple bag-of-balls game, used first to illustrate what a random variable is, then to build its probability distribution, and finally to work out whether the game is profitable for the player or the house. Chapters 2 and 3 stand on their own and do not depend on this example.

How to use these notes: reading in order gives the smoothest path, since later chapters reuse specific numbers and definitions from earlier ones. Each chapter also restates the exact fact it is building on, so you can jump to any single chapter without having to reread everything before it.
